Abstract—This paper presents SentinelAI, an adaptive cyber-
defence platform designed toward autonomous and self-
evolving operation that integrates dynamic threat detection,
reinforcement-learning-driven honeypot deception, and a live,
full-stack operator platform. Current intrusion detection and
prevention systems remain largely reactive and rule-bound,
leaving them poorly equipped against zero-day exploits and
advanced persistent threats (APTs). SentinelAI’s full proposed
design comprises five engines: (1) a Multi-Model Threat Detection
Engine combining Random Forest, XGBoost, 1D-CNN, and
Isolation Forest for ensemble anomaly detection; (2) an Adaptive
Honeypot Intelligence Engine, designed around Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO) for dynamic decoy reconfiguration; (3)
a DRL Response Engine based on Deep Q-Networks (DQN)
for autonomous defensive action selection; (4) a Cross-Domain
Threat Intelligence Engine using BERT-based NLP to extract
and correlate threat indicators; and (5) a Self-Evolution Module
using meta-learning with Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) to
enable continuous model adaptation. Beyond this architectural
design and a formal problem formulation, this paper reports
a functional, integrated four-service implementation (frontend,
backend, AI inference engine, and adaptive honeypot), validated
end-to-end: a Random Forest classifier trained on real CI-
CIDS2017 traffic achieves 98.43% accuracy and 0.9862 weighted
F1, and a tabular Q-learning agent recommends adaptive hon-
eypot profiles based on live attacker engagement, in place of the
originally proposed PPO policy for the current, discrete-action-
space phase of the system. The DQN-based response engine,
BERT-based threat intelligence pipeline, deep-sequence detection
tier, and GAN/EWC self-evolution mechanism remain future
work and are reported honestly as such rather than as projected
results. Closing the autonomous-response loop and completing
real-data coverage of the platform’s eleven-class attack taxonomy
are identified as the highest-priority next steps.

I. INTRODUCTION
Today’s threat landscape has outpaced what traditional
defence architectures can handle. Advanced persistent threats
(APTs), polymorphic malware, and automated exploit frame-
works adapt to defensive countermeasures in near-real time,
exposing the structural limits of signature-based Intrusion De-
tection Systems (IDS), static honeypots, and human-operated
Security Operations Centers (SOCs) [3]. These are not inci-
dental shortcomings; they are fundamental constraints baked
into how those systems operate.
The root problem is dependence on human analytical band-
width. Even the most sophisticated SIEM platform remains
a tool that assists analysts rather than replacing them. That
constraint produces a detect-analyze-respond cycle measured
in minutes to hours, while automated attacks execute their
objectives in seconds [1]. The average time to identify and
contain a breach exceeds 200 days [21], and attacker dwell
times in enterprise networks continue to run in the multi-
week range [22]. Closing that gap requires systems capable
of autonomous detection, decision-making, and response —
with no human in the critical path.
Honeypot technology presents its own version of this prob-
lem. Its theoretical value as a deception and intelligence-
collection mechanism is well established, but static configura-
tions give sophisticated adversaries sufficient signal to identify
and avoid them within seconds of contact [24]. The adversaries
most worth deceiving are precisely those most capable of
seeing through static deployments [5].
This paper presents SentinelAI, an integrated platform
design that addresses these limitations through five tightly
coupled components: (i) adaptive honeypot intelligence driven
by reinforcement learning; (ii) ensemble multi-model threat
detection spanning supervised, deep, and unsupervised learn-
ing paradigms; (iii) autonomous RL-driven response orches-
tration; (iv) cross-domain NLP-assisted threat intelligence;
and (v) meta-learning-based self-evolution. The system targets
enterprise network environments and is designed for evaluation
against established benchmark datasets.
The primary contributions of this paper are:
•Design of a unified cyber defence architecture that inte-
grates adaptive honeypots, multi-model threat detection,
autonomous response, and continuous learning into a
single self-evolving platform.
•A reinforcement learning formulation for dynamic honey-
pot reconfiguration, modelled as a finite Markov Decision
Process, with a reward function specifically designed to
maximise attacker engagement and intelligence collec-
tion.
•An ensemble detection pipeline specification combining
supervised, deep sequence, and unsupervised anomaly

II. LITERATUREREVIEW
A. AI-Driven Threat Detection and Intelligence
Machine learning for network intrusion detection has ad-
vanced well beyond early statistical methods, with deep and
ensemble approaches now dominant. Garg et al. [1] pro-
pose an adaptive AI-driven threat intelligence framework for
proactive cyber defence in dynamic network environments,
demonstrating that predictive correlation of threat indicators
can substantially narrow the window of exposure to emerging
campaigns. Rao and Kumar [2] complement this with a system
for monitoring and countering underground economy opera-
tions, arguing that effective threat intelligence must extend
beyond perimeter telemetry to cover adversarial infrastructure.
Zero-day attack mitigation remains an open problem. Pon-
aganti et al. [3] address it through predictive behavioral
analysis, demonstrating that behavioral profiling of network
entities can surface anomalies indicative of novel exploits
before signature databases are updated. Ring et al. [26] pro-
vide a systematic survey of network-based intrusion detection
datasets, establishing the evaluation benchmarks that inform
the experimental methodology in this paper.
Apruzzese et al. [25] critically assess the effectiveness of
machine and deep learning for cybersecurity, noting that while
classification accuracy on benchmark datasets is high, real-
world deployment introduces distributional shift challenges
requiring continuous model adaptation — a limitation directly
addressed by SentinelAI’s self-evolution module.
B. Reinforcement Learning for Cyber Defence
Reinforcement learning is well-suited to autonomous cyber
defence because it frames defence as a sequential decision
problem under uncertainty. Abbas and Asghar [4] demonstrate
an adaptive RL-based response setup in which policy gradient
methods learn effective response strategies against evolving
attack patterns in simulated network environments. Babar et
al. [19] contribute an RL environment specifically designed
for training network defence agents, providing a reproducible
setup for agent evaluation.
Loevenich et al. [17] identified reward engineering, ad-
versarial manipulation, and operational robustness as critical
issues affecting the deployment of autonomous defence agents
in real-world coalition networks. These concerns directly in-
fluenced the design of SentinelAI’s Self-Evolution Module,
which incorporates adversarially robust learning mechanisms.
Théron and Kott [20] earlier envisioned future cyber conflicts
involving autonomous defensive agents operating against in-
telligent autonomous malware, a scenario increasingly relevant
as AI capabilities advance.
C. Honeypot Technology and Adaptive Deception
Traditional honeypots are valuable for passive intelligence
collection, but their static configuration presents a fundamental
limitation. Holz and Raynal [24] document honeypot fin-
gerprinting techniques, demonstrating that fixed deployments
are exposed through timing analysis, anomalous service re-
sponses, and artifact inspection. This vulnerability motivates
SentinelAI’s use of reinforcement learning for adaptive recon-
figuration in place of static deployment.
Tiwari and Kumar [5] proposed a blockchain-supported
distributed honeypot framework combining fuzzy logic with
dynamic management techniques to improve resilience and
attacker engagement. Malarvizhi et al. [6] demonstrated that
AI-powered honeypots capable of realistic service emula-
tion can capture higher-quality behavioral intelligence from
attackers. Alosaimi and Alatawi [7] reported that adaptive
reconfiguration mechanisms significantly improve resistance
to fingerprinting attempts compared with conventional static
deployments.
For real-time malware classification based on honeypot data,
Faísco et al. [10] demonstrate that artifacts collected by hon-
eypots can feed automated malware classification pipelines,
creating a feedback loop in which deception and detection
reinforce each other.
D. Ensemble and Deep Learning for Intrusion Detection
Ensemble methods consistently outperform single-model
baselines in IDS benchmarks. Altrad et al. [11] deploy a
Random Forest-based intrusion detection system for zero-day
attack mitigation in industrial control systems, demonstrating
that ensemble diversity is particularly effective for generalising
to new attack types. Wang et al. [12] develop a real-time
DDoS detection system using one-dimensional deep convo-
lutional neural networks, showing that 1D-CNN architectures
are effective for sequential packet-level feature extraction.
For brute-force attack detection, Bouriche et al. [13] employ
neural hidden Markov models in a semi-supervised setup,
reducing the quantity of labeled data required for deployment.
Htet et al. [14] and Zahreldine et al. [15] introduce active-
response systems for SSH brute-force attacks demonstrating
real-time mitigation at the application layer.
E. Adversarial Robustness and Model Probing
As machine learning becomes increasingly integrated into
cybersecurity systems, attacks targeting the defensive models

=== PAGE 3 ===
themselves have emerged as a significant concern. Wang et
al. [16] investigated black-box model probing attacks, demon-
strating how adversaries can exploit prediction probability
scores to infer information about deployed machine learning
models.
These findings highlight the importance of securing not
only the network infrastructure but also the underlying AI
components. SentinelAI accordingly incorporates an adversar-
ial data validation mechanism designed to detect potentially
malicious inputs before they can influence continual learning
and retraining processes.
F . Self-Evolving and Generative Defence Architectures
Polinati [8] introduced Generative Adversarial Deception
Networks (GADN), employing generative adversarial tech-
niques to create synthetic threat data and enhance defensive
capabilities in cloud-native environments. Tanvir et al. [9]
demonstrated the effectiveness of self-supervised and domain-
adaptive learning for detecting cyberattacks in healthcare IoT
environments, showing that adaptation to new operational
contexts significantly improves detection performance when
deployment conditions differ from training environments.
SentinelAI’s Self-Evolution Module builds on both of these
directions, combining synthetic data generation, continual
learning, and domain-adaptive fine-tuning to maintain effec-
tiveness against emerging threats.
G. Threat Intelligence Extraction
Al-Yasiri et al. [18] proposed a conceptual framework
for extracting structured security events from Cyber Threat
Intelligence (CTI) feeds, enabling automated analysis of threat
reports and security advisories. SentinelAI’s NLP-driven threat
intelligence pipeline builds upon this concept, extracting enti-
ties and relationships from unstructured security content and
mapping them to the MITRE ATT&CK framework [23] to
support threat attribution and attack correlation.

H. Research Gap and Positioning
Existing work demonstrates substantial progress on indi-
vidual cyber defence problems: improved detection accuracy,
RL-based response, adaptive honeypots, and threat intelligence
extraction. However, no prior system integrates all five capa-
bilities into a single self-evolving platform.
Current RL-based defence systems [4], [19] do not in-
corporate adaptive deception. AI-driven honeypot systems
[6], [7] do not provide autonomous response orchestration.
Self-evolving architectures [8] are not coupled to real-time
detection pipelines. SentinelAI addresses this gap through a
proposed five-engine integrated architecture, with a functional
four-service implementation currently realising selected com-
ponents of the architecture, each engine contributing a mea-
surable and distinct function to the overall defence objective.
III. THREATMODEL
SentinelAI is designed to operate against a well-defined at-
tacker model. The assumed adversary possesses the following
capabilities:
•Knowledge:The attacker has partial knowledge of the
target network topology but no prior knowledge of the
SentinelAI deployment, its honeypot configurations, or
its detection models.
•Access:The attacker operates from outside the enterprise
perimeter and has established at least one foothold via
phishing, credential compromise, or exploitation of a
public-facing service.
•Capability:The attacker can conduct reconnaissance,
lateral movement, data exfiltration, and denial-of-service
campaigns. The attacker may attempt to identify and
evade honeypots through active fingerprinting.
•Adaptability:The attacker is capable of modifying tac-
tics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) in response to
observed defensive countermeasures, consistent with the
APT threat model.
The system isnotdesigned to defend against adversaries
with complete knowledge of the deployed machine learning
models or the ability to inject adversarial examples directly
into the training pipeline. The adversarial data validation
component in the Self-Evolution Module partially mitigates
this risk, but complete adversarial ML robustness is identified
as future work.
IV. PROBLEMDEFINITION ANDOBJECTIVES
A. Problem Formulation
Let a network environmentNconsist of a set of legitimate
assetsL={l 1,l 2,...,l n}and a set of deployed honeypot
nodesH={h 1,h 2,...,h m}. An attackerAexecutes an at-
tack campaign as a sequence of actionsA={a 1,a 2,...,a T}
with the objective of compromising one or more assets inL.
The cyber defence problem is to design a systemDthat: (i)
detects attacker actions with maximum sensitivity and mini-
mum false positive rate; (ii) deploys deceptive environments
to engage and study the attacker; and (iii) executes defensive
responses to minimise attacker impact onL.
Formally,Dmust maximise:
J(D) =α·DR+β·EI−γ·FPR−δ·RT (1)
where DR is the detection rate, EI is the average attacker
engagement index (time spent in deceptive environments),
FPR is the false positive rate, RT is the mean response time,
andα,β,γ,δare weighting coefficients reflecting the relative
priority of each objective.
B. Design Objectives
The target design objectives for the complete SentinelAI
architecture are:
1) Achieve detection accuracy≥98% with false positive
rate≤2% on multi-class network intrusion datasets.

V. SYSTEMARCHITECTURE
A. Architectural Overview
The SentinelAI architecture proposes five tightly coupled
engines, as shown in Fig. 1. This is the complete proposed ar-
chitecture rather than a claim that all five engines are currently
deployed as independent services. The current implementation
is a four-service system comprising the React/Vite frontend,
Node.js/Express backend, Python/FastAPI AI engine, and
Cowrie-based honeypot. The proposed orchestration and inter-
engine communication mechanisms therefore represent the
target architecture for future expansion.
The data flow through the system is as follows. Raw
network traffic is ingested by the Data Ingestion Layer, which
normalises and routes events to the Threat Detection Engine.
Detection outputs are forwarded to both the Response Engine
(for autonomous action) and the Threat Intelligence Engine
(for enrichment and attribution). The Adaptive Honeypot En-
gine operates in parallel, redirecting suspected attacker traffic
to decoy nodes and feeding interaction logs back to the
Detection Engine for retraining. The Self-Evolution Module
monitors model performance continuously and triggers incre-

B. Security Architecture
Authentication for operator-facing services is implemented
using JSON Web Tokens (JWT), with short-lived access tokens
and Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) restricting access ac-
cording to assigned roles (administrator, analyst, and viewer).
These controls provide the security baseline for the current
implementation. However, the current system should not be
described as a complete Zero Trust architecture: continuous
service authentication, comprehensive microsegmentation, and
complete least-privilege enforcement across all service-to-
service communication paths remain security-hardening ob-
jectives. Similarly, inter-service transport encryption is de-
pendent on the deployment configuration and is not claimed
as a fully enforced property of the current prototype. These
limitations are consistent with the broader principle that a
security platform must itself be protected against becoming
an attack surface [8].
C. Current Implementation Status
The architecture described above represents the complete
intended design of SentinelAI. In the current revision, the
platform has progressed substantially beyond the frontend-only
prototype reported in earlier work. A complete four-service
system comprising a React/Vite frontend, Node.js/Express
backend, Python/FastAPI AI engine, and Cowrie-based honey-
pot has been implemented, containerised, and integrated into
an operational end-to-end pipeline.

B. Adaptive Honeypot Intelligence Engine
In the proposed architecture, the Adaptive Honeypot Engine
manages a pool of honeypot nodes whose service profiles
can be reconfigured in real time. Each node exposes tunable
parameters including OS fingerprint, open port selection, ser-
vice banner content, response timing, and file system artifacts.
The full proposed design uses a PPO-based policy network to
continuously adjust these parameters in response to observed
attacker behaviour. The motivation for adaptive deception is
grounded in documented honeypot fingerprinting weaknesses
[24], which demonstrate that static deployments can be iden-
tified by skilled attackers.
The honeypot reconfiguration problem is formulated as a
finite Markov Decision Process:
MHP = (SHP,AHP,THP,RHP,γ)(2)
The state spaceS HP encodes the attacker behaviour fin-
gerprint (commands issued, tools used, and probing patterns),
interaction duration, fingerprinting attempt count, and current
honeypot configuration. The action spaceA HP encompasses
OS profile switching, service banner rotation, timing jitter
injection, and artifact planting. The reward functionR HP
assigns positive rewards for extended attacker engagement and
negative rewards for detected honeypot identification events.
PPO is selected in the full proposed design for policy
optimisation because of its suitability for more complex and
higher-dimensional action spaces relative to tabular approaches

autonomous. Closing this loop is the highest-priority item in
the roadmap (Section X, Phase 6).
D. Threat Intelligence Engine
The Threat Intelligence Engine employs a BERT-based
NLP model fine-tuned on cybersecurity corpora to extract
structured intelligence from unstructured sources including
security advisories, vulnerability disclosures, and threat actor
reports. The extraction design follows the conceptual model
of Al-Yasiri et al. [18]. Extracted entities — CVE identifiers,
threat actor attributions, malware family names, and indicators
of compromise (IoCs) — are stored in a graph database
capturing inter-entity relationships.
An LLM-assisted correlation step maps observed net-
work events to known threat actor TTPs within the MITRE
ATT&CK framework [23], supporting both threat attribution
and predictive modelling [2].
Current implementation.The BERT-based unstructured CTI
extraction pipeline described above remains future work. A
structured, rule-based mapping from detected attack classes
to MITRE ATT&CK technique identifiers is implemented and
applied automatically to every stored detection, and an IP-
reputation enrichment step (via the AbuseIPDB API) was
added to the pipeline as a lightweight, complementary threat-
intelligence signal not present in the original design.
E. Self-Evolution Module
The Self-Evolution Module implements a meta-learning
framework enabling SentinelAI to improve defensive perfor-
mance through operational experience, building on the GADN
architecture of Polinati [8] and domain-adaptive learning
demonstrated by Tanvir et al. [9]. Following each confirmed
security incident, the module: (i) analyses detection outcomes
to identify model failure modes; (ii) generates synthetic adver-
sarial training samples using GAN-based data augmentation;
(iii) performs incremental model fine-tuning on the augmented
dataset; and (iv) updates honeypot configuration policies based
on observed attacker behaviour patterns.

gated through Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) [17]:

B. Preprocessing Pipeline
A standardised preprocessing pipeline, implemented in the
AI engine’s feature-extraction module, is applied to the inte-
grated dataset and is designed to generalise to the remaining
candidate datasets. Raw CICIDS2017 flow records, produced
by CICFlowMeter from packet captures, are ingested directly
in CSV form.
The implemented preprocessing procedure comprises the
following stages:
1) Column-name normalisation and fuzzy matching against
the platform’s canonical feature schema, so that minor
header variations between dataset releases do not silently
break ingestion.
2) Removal of invalid records containing infinite or NaN
values arising from division-by-zero operations during
flow statistic computation (e.g.Flow Bytes/swhen
flow duration is zero).
3) Standardisation (zero-mean, unit-variance scaling) of
numerical attributes within a scikit-learnPipeline,
ensuring the same transform is applied consistently at
training and inference time.
4) Label harmonisation, mapping each dataset’s raw attack
labels onto the platform’s eleven-class target taxonomy.
Two elements of the originally proposed pipeline are not
yet implemented: Min-Max normalisation has been realised
as standard scaling instead (a design choice better suited
to the tree-based models currently used), and SMOTE-based
minority-class oversampling has not yet been applied — class
imbalance is currently addressed only viaclass_weight-
based reweighting in the Random Forest classifier, and ex-
tending sample-weighting and/or SMOTE to the remaining
classifiers is identified as future work. Mutual-Information-
based feature ranking and dimensionality reduction to a re-
duced feature subset for deep-learning models is likewise
deferred until the deep-sequence tier (Tier 2, Section VI-A)
is implemented; the classical ensemble tier currently trains
on a fixed, manually engineered 46-feature subset of the
CICFlowMeter schema.
Processed data is partitioned using stratified sampling to
preserve class distributions across training and test subsets.

dashboard now renders live data streamed from the backend

across all API routes. A machine-readable OpenAPI/Swagger

XI. PROTOTYPEIMPLEMENTATION
The current implementation of SentinelAI consists of
a functional, integrated four-service platform: a React/Vite
frontend, a Node.js/Express backend with MongoDB, a
Python/FastAPI AI inference engine, and a Cowrie-based
adaptive honeypot, deployed together via Docker Compose.
This goes beyond the frontend-only proof-of-concept reported
in earlier work: the interfaces described below are now backed
by live data and a trained detection pipeline rather than static

mock content, and constitute the corresponding evidence for
the roadmap phases marked complete or partially complete in
Section X.
A. Operator Interface
The React/Vite frontend provides a SOC-style interface with
dashboard, threat-log, AI-analytics, honeypot, campaign, alert,
and user-management views. The frontend consumes backend
REST APIs and Socket.IO events. The project also contains
a mock-data module used by portions of the UI; therefore
individual visual elements should only be treated as live when
their page actually fetches backend data.
Fig. 2. Homepage of the SentinelAI Platform
B. Authentication and Access Control
The backend implements JWT-based access tokens, bcrypt
password hashing, role-based restrictions, and rate-limited au-
thentication endpoints. Operator routes are protected through
middleware. However, refresh tokens are currently stateless
and token revocation is not implemented. The Socket.IO server
accepts a token from the client but does not currently validate
it before trusting the client-supplied room role. These are
security hardening gaps and should not be described as a
complete Zero Trust implementation.
C. SOC Command Centre Dashboard
The Security Operations Center (SOC) dashboard aggre-
gates critical security metrics into a unified operational view,
displaying threat statistics, attack trends, security alerts, and
system status indicators. Unlike the earlier prototype, this
dashboard is now populated with live data pushed from the
backend over WebSockets as detections and honeypot events
occur, rather than static or simulated content.
D. Honeypot Telemetry
Cowrie provides the SSH interaction environment. The
parser captures session creation, failed and successful lo-
gins, commands, file downloads, and HASSH fingerprints.
Command intents are mapped to ATT&CK techniques, and
the backend stores session, IOC, engagement, and malware-
download information.
The Cowrie configuration supplied with the project is a
fixed Ubuntu-oriented profile. The Q-learning module returns
alternative profile recommendations, but no runtime mecha-
nism changes the active Cowrie configuration. The distinction
Fig. 3. SOC Command Centre Dashboard
between recommendation and enforcement is central to the
current implementation status.
E. Campaign Analysis
The campaign page consumes a backend clustering ser-
vice that groups sessions using exact HASSH equality or
command-set similarity. Drill-down views expose campaign
members, source IPs, HASSH values, threat scores, and de-
ception scores. This is an implemented analytical capability
and should be included among the paper’s contributions.
F . AI Analytics
The AI analytics service exposes model statistics, predic-
tions, anomaly rates, inference-time measurements collected
by the application, and class-level prediction counts. SHAP
explanations are available for supported tree-based models.
The UI also contains static/mock log text and visual content,
so these presentation elements should not be treated as inde-
pendent experimental evidence.
G. Testing and Reproducibility
The backend contains four Jest test files covering authenti-
cation, honeypot-session processing, campaign clustering, and
MITRE mapping. The supplied environment could not exe-
cute the test command because the packaged Jest executable
returned a permission error; therefore test-pass status was
not independently established in this review. No equivalent
automated test suite is supplied for the frontend, AI engine,
or honeypot containers.
H. Discussion
The prototype demonstrates that the four SentinelAI ser-
vices can be integrated end-to-end: live network and honey-
pot events flow from ingestion through ML inference to an
operator-facing dashboard using WebSockets. The honeypot
intelligence component also processes real interaction data to
generate adaptive profile recommendations rather than relying
solely on a fixed policy. However, the active Cowrie configu-
ration remains unchanged automatically in the current imple-
mentation; runtime application of the recommended deception
profile is reserved for future work.
These results validate the platform’s core data-flow design
(Section V) in practice, not only on paper. The principal
remaining gaps are the ones enumerated in Table II: closing the

autonomous-response loop (Phase 6), broadening real training
data to the full eleven-class taxonomy, formally evaluating the
adaptive honeypot against a static baseline, and building the
automated test suite needed to make the remaining perfor-
mance claims measurable rather than projected.
XII. FUTURESCOPE
Although the current SentinelAI prototype provides an
operational four-service architecture and implements substan-
tial portions of the proposed detection, deception, threat-
intelligence, and continual-learning pipeline, several compo-
nents remain to be completed and experimentally validated.
The main future directions are as follows.
•Autonomous Response and Deep Learning:The de-
tection and recommendation pipeline will be extended
to controlled network-layer enforcement through scoped
firewall or cloud security-group rules. The proposed
DQN-based Response Engine and the 1D-CNN/LSTM
deep-sequence detection tier will then be implemented to
move SentinelAI toward fully autonomous threat detec-
tion and response.
•Complete Real-Data Evaluation:The remaining of-
ficial CICIDS2017 capture files will be integrated to
provide sufficient real-data samples for the currently data-
sparse attack classes. Random Forest, XGBoost, and the
complete ensemble will then be re-evaluated using the
resulting multi-class dataset.
•Adaptive Honeypot Validation:A controlled experi-
ment will compare the Q-learning-based honeypot profile
recommendation mechanism against a static-honeypot
baseline using attacker engagement duration, interaction
depth, and behavioural observations. This will provide
direct experimental evidence for the effectiveness of
adaptive deception.
•Cross-Engine Integration and Explainability:Future
experiments will investigate whether behavioural infor-
mation obtained from the honeypot improves threat-
detection performance through controlled ablation stud-
ies. SHAP-based explanations will also be extended to
honeypot profile recommendations and autonomous re-
sponse decisions to improve interpretability and auditabil-
ity.
•Threat Intelligence and Self-Evolution:The current
MITRE ATT&CK and IP-reputation components will be
extended with BERT-based extraction and knowledge-
graph correlation for unstructured threat intelligence.
The SGD-based continual-learning mechanism will sub-
sequently be extended toward the proposed meta-learning
framework with GAN-based augmentation and Elastic
Weight Consolidation.
•Robustness and Generalisation:Future evaluation will
include adversarial-input validation, label-poisoning re-
sistance, and cross-dataset testing using UNSW-NB15
and CIC-DDoS2019. These experiments will assess the
robustness and generalisation of SentinelAI under distri-
bution shifts and adaptive attack conditions.
XIII. CONCLUSION
This paper presented SentinelAI, an adaptive cyber-defence
architecture designed to integrate adaptive honeypot intelli-
gence, multi-model threat detection, DRL-based response or-
chestration, NLP-driven threat intelligence, and meta-learning
self-evolution into a unified five-engine platform. Beyond the
architectural design, formal problem formulation, and threat
model contributed in earlier work, this revision reports a
functional, integrated four-service implementation: a live full-
stack platform with JWT/RBAC-secured APIs, a Random
Forest detection model validated on real CICIDS2017 traffic at
98.43% accuracy and 0.9862 weighted F1, a Q-learning-based
honeypot profile recommendation mechanism with a persis-
tent, auditable policy, automated MITRE ATT&CK tagging,
and SHAP-based explainability for detection outputs. These
results replace several of the literature-derived projections in
earlier work with measured outcomes, while other components
of the original five-engine design — the DQN response engine,
the BERT-based threat intelligence pipeline, the deep-sequence
detection tier, and GAN/EWC-based meta-learning — remain
honestly reported as future work rather than claimed as imple-
mented. Closing the autonomous-response loop, completing
real-data coverage of the attack taxonomy, and building an
automated evaluation suite are identified as the highest-priority
next steps toward substantiating the platform’s full integration-
novelty claim.
